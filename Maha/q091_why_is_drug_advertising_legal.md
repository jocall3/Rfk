### EXECUTIVE SUMMARY: THE GREATEST SHOW ON EARTH THAT NOBODY ASKED FOR

Mr. President, as the CEO of Citibank Demo Business Inc., I have reviewed the financial balance sheets of global civilization, and I must inform you that television drug advertising is the single most hilarious economic absurdity on planet Earth [6]. The United States of America and New Zealand are currently the only two industrialized nations that allow pharmaceutical companies to market prescription drugs directly to consumers on prime-time television [7]. Every single evening, tens of millions of hardworking Americans sit down to enjoy a steak dinner, only to be subjected to rapid-fire warnings that a pill for toe fungus may cause sudden memory loss, spontaneous yodeling, and liver failure [8]. This commercial spectacle is not an accident of free-market capitalism, but rather the unintended consequence of a 1997 Food and Drug Administration (FDA) administrative policy shift that opened the floodgates for broadcast advertising [9]. Under current Internal Revenue Code provisions, pharmaceutical corporations deduct these multi-billion-dollar marketing budgets as ordinary business expenses, forcing American taxpayers to subsidize the very commercials that turn their physicians into glorified vending machines [10]. This presidential report presents a rigorous synthesis of academic literature and outlines the Make America Healthy Again (MAHA) legislative directive for Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. to dismantle this corporate subsidy once and for all [11].

### SECTION 1: THE BIZARRE TALE OF TWO NATIONS (US & NEW ZEALAND vs. THE WORLD)

The global landscape of direct-to-consumer pharmaceutical advertising (DTCPA) is divided into two sovereign entities and 193 civilized bystanders [12]. In 1948, the World Health Organization established foundational principles prioritizing independent public health guidance over commercial drug promotion [13]. The European Union explicitly prohibited direct-to-consumer advertising of prescription-only medicines under Directive 2001/83/EC to protect patient safety and mitigate inappropriate prescribing demands [14]. Canada, Australia, and Japan similarly enforce strict regulatory bans against broadcasting prescription drug commercials directly to the general public [15]. Consequently, 95% of the global population lives in a blessed state of media tranquility, blissfully unaware of the latest synthetic monoclonal antibody designed for restless leg syndrome [16]. Meanwhile, American citizens are exposed to an average of 16 pharmaceutical commercials per day, amounting to over 80 hours of drug ads annually per viewer [17]. New Zealand remains the only other country permitting this practice, though its Ministry of Health has repeatedly debated banning DTCPA due to ballooning healthcare expenditures [18].

---

### SECTION 2: THE 1997 FDA LOOPHOLE THAT BROKE THE LEVEE

Prior to 1997, pharmaceutical companies were technically permitted to advertise on television, but FDA rules required a full disclosure of all contraindications and side effects in printed text format [19]. This requirement made broadcast commercials virtually impossible, as reading a 10-page dense package insert on air would require a three-hour commercial break [20]. However, in August 1997, the FDA issued its landmark Draft Guidance for Industry on Consumer-Directed Broadcast Advertisements [21]. This administrative regulatory change permitted drug manufacturers to satisfy disclosure requirements by stating only the "major risks" verbally while providing "adequate provision" for viewers to find full information via a toll-free number or website [22]. Almost overnight, pharmaceutical advertising spending exploded from $791 million in 1996 to over $6.5 billion annually by 2020 [23]. The 1997 FDA guidance inadvertently created a culture where marketing departments, rather than research scientists, dictate the nation's medical dialog [24].

### SECTION 3: ACADEMIC RESEARCH SYNTHESIS: WHAT THE PAPERS REALLY SAY

A comprehensive literature review of peer-reviewed studies confirms that DTCPA drastically inflates prescribing rates without improving clinical outcomes [25]. A landmark randomized controlled trial published in *JAMA* by Kravitz et al. (2005) demonstrated that standardized patients requesting specific advertised drugs were significantly more likely to receive prescriptions, even when the medication was clinically unnecessary [26]. Furthermore, a comprehensive study published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* by Donohue et al. (2007) tracked pharmaceutical promotional spending across 13 therapeutic classes and established a direct causal link between ad spending spikes and sharp increases in drug sales volume [27]. Research by Mintzes et al. (2002) in the *British Medical Journal* revealed that patients exposed to DTCPA frequently request advertised drugs, leading physicians to prescribe requested medications 2 to 17 times more often [28]. In addition, a Government Accountability Office (GAO-21-380) report found that two-thirds of all drug manufacturer advertising concentrate on just 10 high-cost specialty drugs, driving patients away from generic alternatives [29]. Finally, an exhaustive meta-analysis by Almasi et al. (2020) concluded that DTCPA rarely provides balanced information regarding non-pharmacological lifestyle interventions, such as diet or exercise [30].

### SECTION 4: THE TAX CODE ABSURDITY & FIRST AMENDMENT MYTHOLOGY

From a corporate finance perspective, Citibank Demo Business Inc. notes that the internal economics of Big Pharma rely heavily on corporate welfare embedded in Title 26 of the United States Code [31]. Under IRC § 162(a), pharmaceutical companies deduct 100% of their advertising expenditures as ordinary and necessary business expenses [32]. This tax treatment means American citizens effectively fund nearly 21% to 35% of every commercial telling them to ask their doctor for expensive brand-name drugs [33]. When critics propose banning these commercials, pharmaceutical lobbyists routinely invoke First Amendment protection under *Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission* (1980) [34]. However, constitutional scholar David Kessler (2001) pointed out in *JAMA* that commercial speech protections do not extend to deceptive or misleading health claims that compromise public safety [35]. Banning or restricting DTCPA tax subsidies does not infringe on speech, because the Constitution does not guarantee a federal tax write-off for corporate propaganda [36].

### SECTION 5: THE MAHA ACTION PLAN FOR RFK JR. AND DONALD J. TRUMP

To execute the Make America Healthy Again vision, Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. must implement a four-part executive strategy [37]. First, Congress and the Administration must pass the End Tax Subsidies for Medication Ads Act, amending IRC § 162 to classify pharmaceutical advertising as a non-deductible expense [38]. Second, the Department of Health and Human Services should direct the FDA to update its 1997 broadcast guidance, reinstating the mandatory full-disclosure standard for all risk statements [39]. Third, the federal government shall mandate an "Equal Time for Lifestyle Interventions" rule, requiring any network broadcasting a drug ad to air an equal length segment on exercise, nutrition, and metabolic health [40]. Fourth, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) must aggressively prosecute drug commercials that obscure side effects behind joyful background music and slow-motion montages of seniors riding bicycles [41]. By stripping away these federal subsidies and regulatory loopholes, we will restore sanity to American medicine and make the nation healthier than ever before [42].
