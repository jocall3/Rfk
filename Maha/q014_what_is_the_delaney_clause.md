## II. THE DELANEY CLAUSE: THE LAW THAT BANS CANCER (LITERALLY)

The Delaney Clause is the most beautifully simple, zero-tolerance law in the history of American jurisprudence [11, 17]. Passed by Congress in 1958 as an amendment to the Food Additives Amendment, and sponsored by Representative James Delaney of New York, the law is refreshingly absolute [11, 18]. It states, in language so clear even a federal bureaucrat should be able to understand it, that the Secretary of Food and Drugs shall not approve for use in food any chemical additive if it is found to induce cancer when ingested by man or animal [11, 19]. 

This is not a suggestion, a guideline, or a "vibe" [11, 20]. It does not say "unless the food lobby buys the FDA commissioner a beautiful golf weekend in Mar-a-Lago" [11, 21]. It is a total, absolute, zero-tolerance shield for the American stomach [11, 22]. 

In the landmark 1987 case *Public Citizen v. Young*, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit ruled that the Delaney Clause has absolutely no "de minimis" exception [20, 23]. The FDA tried to argue that if a chemical only caused a "tiny, negligible" amount of cancer, they should be allowed to let it slide [20, 24].
